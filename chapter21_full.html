Q266 What is the full cost of adopting, operating, maintaining, and eventually replacing or leaving an AI system in an educational institution?

7 citations · 7 sources · 4 cross-references

Claim 1 emerging

The full cost of an educational AI system is the sum of everything an institution must spend, and every hour its people must give, from the first evaluation of a product to the day its data has been retrieved and the service switched off. The licence fee is the most visible part of that sum and often not the largest. On the evidence currently available, the honest answer has two halves. The categories of cost are well understood and are now set out in established total-cost-of-ownership (TCO) frameworks. The proportions — how much of the total each category represents in a typical school or university — are not reliably established, and several widely repeated percentages cannot be traced to published primary research. An institution can therefore know with confidence what to count, but must measure its own figures rather than borrow someone else's.

Scope and terms. This answer uses "AI system" to mean any externally supplied or institutionally built tool that relies on machine-learning models — most commonly, today, general-purpose generative AI assistants and education-specific tutoring, feedback, or administrative products. Total cost of ownership is the full lifecycle cost of a technology rather than its purchase price; the Consortium for School Networking (CoSN), the US association of school technology leaders, groups it into hardware and software, infrastructure, support and maintenance, training, and indirect labour — "staff time spent troubleshooting and managing disruptions" [SRC-446]. The examples below are drawn mainly from the United States, where most published procurement figures originate; funding rules, labour costs, and data-protection obligations differ elsewhere, and the figures should not be transferred to other jurisdictions without adjustment. K–12 and higher education are distinguished where the difference matters.

Five distinctions that make the total visible. A full cost account separates five things that budget presentations routinely merge. Initial expenditure (evaluation, procurement, legal review, integration, set-up, launch training) is different from recurring cost (subscriptions, usage charges, support, renewal, refresher training). Direct cost — what appears on an invoice — is different from indirect cost, chiefly staff time that is real but not separately budgeted. Cost borne by the institution is different from cost shifted to others: to teachers who learn a tool in their own time, to students and families who pay for what the institution does not supply (Q270, Q271), and to neighbouring budgets such as networking or devices. Expected cost is different from contingent cost — price rises at renewal, emergency fixes, or the expense of leaving a vendor. And the cost of a licence is different from the cost of use: a price quoted per licensed user says little about the price per person who actually uses the tool. Each distinction exposes a part of the total that a single headline figure hides.

Adoption: the costs before the first lesson. Before any student or teacher uses an AI system, an institution has already spent money and time. Products must be evaluated against an educational purpose and against alternatives; contracts must be reviewed for data protection, ownership of inputs and outputs, model-training rights, security obligations, and exit terms; systems must be connected to identity management, learning platforms, and student information systems; and staff must be trained before launch. These costs are largely staff time — procurement officers, lawyers, technology teams, curriculum leaders — and therefore rarely appear as a line item. In the United States, the Department of Education's July 2025 guidance confirms that federal formula and discretionary grant funds may be used for AI-powered instructional materials, tutoring, and advising, including training for educators, and it reminds grantees that such uses must comply with federal privacy law, including the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act [SRC-1673]. Compliance of this kind is not free: it requires review of vendors' data practices and, often, negotiated contract terms. The guidance makes certain spending lawful; it does not pay for the work needed to make a particular product compliant.

Operation: subscriptions, usage, and the infrastructure underneath. Once in service, an AI system carries recurring charges. Most institutional products are sold per user per year; some charge by usage, so costs rise with adoption; many bundle AI features into existing platforms, which makes the AI share of a larger renewal difficult to isolate. Underneath the subscription sits infrastructure the institution must already maintain or must now upgrade. A practitioner guide to the cost of student devices sets out five cost areas that districts should include — acquisition; software and management; deployment and operations, including staff labour; repair and downtime, including the value of lost instructional time; and retirement, including data sanitisation and disposal [SRC-1674]. The guide concerns devices rather than AI, but AI tools run on those devices and networks, and an AI programme that assumes one capable device per student inherits the full lifecycle cost of that assumption. The book's earlier analysis of system-level cost (Q122) reaches the same conclusion: institutions that do not count infrastructure systematically understate what AI costs them.

Maintenance: support, training, and staff time. AI systems change more often than conventional software. Models are updated, features appear and disappear, and usage policies shift. Each change creates work: help-desk support, revised guidance, refresher training, checks that safeguards still behave as intended, and responses to incidents such as inaccurate outputs or inappropriate content. CoSN's inclusion of indirect labour in its framework [SRC-446] is especially relevant here, because the largest maintenance cost is usually the time of teachers and support staff, which is spread across many people in small amounts and is almost never measured. An institution that records only invoices will see a stable cost while its staff absorb a growing one.

Price and its instability: the California State University case. The most detailed public example of institutional AI pricing shows how quickly the headline number can move. In early 2025, the CSU Chancellor's Office signed an 18-month contract (February 2025–July 2026) providing up to 500,000 ChatGPT Edu licences to students and employees [SRC-1675]; the cost was reported as about $17 million [SRC-1676]. In May 2026 the system renewed for three years at $13 million a year, $39 million in total, with an annual option to cancel on advance notice [SRC-1677]. CSU states that the renewed agreement costs $1.60 per user, that the next-closest bid was $8.00 per user per month, and that coverage will expand from 500,000 to 675,000 users, including graduates for a year after graduation [SRC-1675]. Dividing $13 million by 675,000 users and twelve months gives roughly $1.60, which suggests the figure is per user per month at full projected enrolment — an inference from the published numbers, not a disclosed contract term.

Three lessons follow. First, the per-user price fell on renewal while the total remained large, so "price per user" and "total commitment" can move in different directions. Second, the price per licensed user is not the price per active user. CSU's published figures describe eligible users; this research cycle found no disclosed figures on how many of them use the tool regularly, and without those figures the cost per person actually served cannot be calculated. If half the eligible users never log in, the effective price per user doubles. Third, contract structure is itself a cost-control instrument. A multi-year agreement with an annual cancellation option limits the institution's exposure if prices, products, or priorities change. The renewal was also contested: EdSource reported faculty opposition, with critics arguing that the money should be weighed against jobs and other needs on campuses facing budget pressure [SRC-1676]. That debate is a reminder that the full cost of an AI system includes its opportunity cost — what the same money would otherwise have bought (Q267).

Replacement and exit: the cost most often left out. Every AI system will eventually be replaced, renegotiated, or abandoned, and the cost of leaving is set largely when the contract is signed. Exit costs include retrieving and converting institutional data; confirming deletion of student data held by the vendor; rebuilding integrations; retraining staff on a successor product; rewriting course materials, assessments, and guidance that assumed the old tool; and running two systems in parallel during transition. Where a vendor has used institutional data to configure or tune its service, the configuration may not be portable at all. Contracts that specify data-return formats, deletion timelines, transition assistance, and notice periods reduce these costs; contracts that are silent on them transfer the risk to the institution. The book's procurement question (Q073) sets out the relevant contract terms; this answer adds that exit should be priced at the outset, as a contingent liability, rather than discovered at renewal.

The circulating percentages, and why they should not be used. Precise-sounding proportions are often repeated in commentary on AI costs: that licence fees represent only 40–50 per cent of total cost of ownership, that institutions underestimate the total by 40–60 per cent, and that AI subscription prices rise by around 12 per cent a year. A vendor blog post on the economics of AI in education, rated low credibility in this book, is one source that presents these figures [SRC-1678]. This research cycle could not trace any of them to published primary research — a multi-institution study with a stated method and sample. They may be broadly right; they may not be. Either way, they cannot bear the weight of a budget decision. The direction they point in is supported by the frameworks above, which consistently show that recurring, indirect, and contingent costs are substantial [SRC-446] [SRC-1674]. Their specific magnitudes are not established.

K–12 and higher education. The cost structure differs by sector. School districts typically buy for children, which raises the bar for data protection and parental consent, and they depend heavily on grant and categorical funding whose rules shape what can be bought and for how long [SRC-1673]. Universities more often buy for adults at very large scale, as the CSU case shows, and face distinctive costs in research data governance, academic-integrity processes, and support for graduates and staff. In both sectors, however, the largest uncertainties are the same: actual uptake, staff time, and the price at renewal.

What a credible cost account contains. An institution deciding whether to adopt, renew, or replace an AI system can apply six criteria. It should estimate cost over the whole expected life of the system, including exit, not over the first contract year. It should report cost per active user alongside cost per licence, and collect the usage data needed to do so. It should record staff time for implementation, support, and training, even approximately, rather than treating it as free. It should identify costs shifted to students, families, and staff. It should compare the system against at least one non-AI alternative and against doing nothing. And it should treat unsourced industry percentages as hypotheses to test against local data, not as planning assumptions. Finally, it should write down and publish the assumptions, so that the analysis can be checked and revisited. The full cost of AI is knowable in structure; what institutions lack is the habit of counting all of it.

Q267 How should educational institutions finance AI, and how should they weigh that expenditure against competing educational needs?

7 citations · 7 sources · 8 cross-references

Claim 1 emerging

There is no single correct way to fund educational AI, but there is a defensible order of reasoning. Institutions should first decide what educational or operational problem a tool is meant to solve and what non-AI alternatives could solve it; then match the funding source to the nature of the cost — recurring commitments to recurring money, experiments to temporary money; and only then decide whether the expenditure is a better use of scarce resources than the alternatives it displaces. The available evidence does not establish that AI spending, in general, produces more educational value per dollar than spending on teachers, tutoring, disability support, or materials. Nor does it establish the opposite. What it does establish is that the value of AI is specific to the use, the design, and the setting, which means financing decisions must be made use by use rather than for "AI" as a category.

Scope and terms. This answer concerns publicly funded schools and universities, with most examples from the United States and England, where funding rules and published evidence are most accessible. "Financing" means the choice of money used to pay for AI; "weighing" means the comparison of AI spending with other uses of the same money. The full cost to be financed is set out in Q266; the evidence on what AI returns is set out in Q268; the costs that fall on households and students are examined in Q270 and Q271. Funding rules differ across jurisdictions, and nothing below should be read as legal advice on the use of any particular fund.

Step one: the problem before the product. The most common financing error happens before money is discussed at all: a product is chosen, and a budget is then sought for it. A defensible process runs the other way. It names the problem — teacher planning load, tutoring capacity, feedback turnaround, administrative backlog — and the outcome that would show the problem has been reduced. It identifies at least one non-AI way of addressing the same problem, and the option of doing nothing. Only then does it ask whether an AI tool is likely to do better. This sequence matters financially because it converts an open-ended technology purchase into a bounded comparison between alternatives, each with a cost and an expected effect.

Step two: matching money to the nature of the cost. Institutions typically draw on several kinds of money, each suited to a different kind of cost.

Temporary and one-time money. Grants, philanthropic gifts, and short-term programme funds are suited to pilots, evaluation, implementation, and initial training — costs that end. In the United States, the Department of Education's July 2025 guidance confirms that federal formula and discretionary grant funds may be used for AI-based instructional materials, tutoring, and advising, provided programme requirements are met and privacy law is respected [SRC-1673]. That guidance creates a lawful route; it does not create new money, and every dollar of an existing grant spent on AI is a dollar not spent on that grant's other allowable uses.

Recurring operating funds. Ongoing licences, support, and refresher training are permanent commitments and belong in the recurring budget. This is where trade-offs are sharpest, because recurring funds also pay salaries.

Vendor-subsidised or bundled access. Free tiers, discounted introductory pricing, and AI features bundled into existing platforms lower the initial cost, but price, data rights, and dependence remain, and pricing can change at renewal.

Tuition and mandatory fees. Particularly in higher education, AI costs can be built into tuition or technology fees. This spreads costs across all students, including those who do not use the tool.

User charges. Institutions can require individuals to buy their own access. CSU's own analysis described this as the alternative to systemwide purchase: the next-closest provider "would need to be supported by campus resources or a user fee" [SRC-1675]. User charges move costs from the institution to households and students, with the equity consequences examined in Q270 and Q271.

Matching money to commitment. The most common structural error is financing recurring obligations with temporary money. A pilot funded by a one-year grant or a vendor discount creates users, habits, and expectations; when the grant ends, the institution faces a choice between finding recurring money under pressure and withdrawing a tool that staff and students now rely on. Either outcome is costly. The safer rule is that temporary money may start something only if the institution has identified, in advance, the recurring money that will continue it or the conditions under which it will be stopped. Institutions that adopted technology with time-limited pandemic-era funds faced this problem at scale, and the same risk applies to AI.

Allocating fairly: formulas and who gets access. How money is distributed within a system matters as much as where it comes from. A district or university that funds AI through individual school or departmental budgets will see uneven adoption, because better-resourced units can afford licences, training, and support while others cannot. Allocating AI funding by a per-student or per-staff formula, or providing core tools centrally, protects against that drift, at the cost of buying access for units that may not use it. The book's equity chapter shows how readily technology spending widens existing gaps, including between rural and urban schools (Q190), and which interventions have reduced them (Q193). A financing plan should therefore state not only how much will be spent, but how access will be distributed and how the distribution will be checked.

Renewal as a financing decision. Initial adoption receives most scrutiny, but renewal is where most AI money is committed. A renewal should be treated as a new decision rather than an administrative continuation: has the tool been used, by whom, with what measured effect, at what cost per active user, and what would the money buy instead? Renewals approved during budget reductions deserve particular attention, because they signal that AI spending has been protected while other spending has been cut. That may be justified, but the justification should be explicit. Contract terms that allow annual cancellation, of the kind CSU negotiated [SRC-1675], make such reviews meaningful; multi-year commitments without exit options make them largely symbolic.

The California State University example. CSU's systemwide agreement with OpenAI illustrates both the advantages and the risks of central financing. The renewed agreement costs $1.60 per user, against a next-closest bid of $8.00 per user per month, and extends coverage to 675,000 students, staff, and recent graduates [SRC-1675]. Central purchasing achieved a unit price far below consumer list prices and avoided user fees. It also created a large recurring commitment from a public budget during a period of financial pressure, which faculty critics opposed on the grounds that the money was needed elsewhere [SRC-1676]. Neither side of that dispute could point to published outcome data showing what the licence had achieved in its first period. That absence is the central financing lesson: a recurring commitment of this size should come with a plan to measure what it buys.

Step three: weighing AI against competing needs. Once a funding source is identified, the expenditure must be compared with what else the money could do. The relevant comparison is not between AI and nothing, but between AI and the next-best use of the same funds — additional tutoring hours, smaller groups, specialist support, curriculum materials, or staff time.

The available evidence allows only a partial comparison. The strongest experimental evidence on teacher time comes from the Education Endowment Foundation's trial in English secondary schools, in which science teachers using ChatGPT with a guide reduced weekly lesson-planning time by about 31 per cent, with no apparent loss of quality in the resources produced [SRC-1650] [SRC-1176]. That is a real but bounded effect: about 25 minutes a week on one task. Survey evidence from the United States suggests larger self-reported savings among regular users. Gallup's research with the Walton Family Foundation found that the roughly three in ten teachers who used AI at least weekly estimated saving about 5.9 hours a week, equivalent to about six weeks over a school year [SRC-1286] [SRC-1679]. These are self-reports from adopters rather than measured effects, and they should not be treated as equivalent to the trial result. Walton also reported that teachers in schools with an AI policy reported larger time savings than those in schools without one [SRC-1679], which suggests that the return on AI spending depends partly on the spending around it — on guidance, training, and policy.

None of these studies converts time saved into money saved, and none compares AI directly with an alternative use of the same budget. An institution weighing AI against a teaching post, a tutoring programme, or disability support must therefore make an explicit judgement, using local evidence, rather than appeal to a general finding that AI "pays for itself".

Public opinion and legitimacy. Financing decisions are also judged by the public and by staff. Survey evidence shows a teaching workforce divided between regular users, occasional users, and non-users [SRC-1286], which means that any substantial AI expenditure will be seen by part of the workforce as a diversion from priorities they consider more urgent. A decision process that is transparent about alternatives, costs, and expected outcomes is more likely to be accepted than one that presents AI spending as self-evidently modern or efficient.

Practical implications. A defensible financing decision for any AI tool meets seven criteria. It names the problem and the outcome before the product. It compares at least one non-AI alternative and the option of doing nothing. It funds recurring costs from recurring money, and uses temporary money only with a plan for continuation or exit. It costs the full lifecycle, including exit (Q266). It identifies who bears costs not paid by the institution. It specifies in advance how effect will be measured and when the decision will be reviewed. And it records the reasoning in board or governing-body papers, so that the choice can be scrutinised and revisited.

Q268 What evidence would establish whether an educational AI investment delivers financial savings, productivity gains, educational value, or a worthwhile combination of these outcomes?

10 citations · 10 sources · 4 cross-references

Claim 1 emerging

An educational AI investment can be said to deliver value only when four separate questions have been answered, and the evidence for each is different. Has spending actually fallen somewhere (a financial saving)? Has staff or student time been freed or capacity expanded (a productivity gain)? Have learning, access, or wellbeing improved (educational value)? And, taking full costs into account, is the combination better than the realistic alternatives (return on investment or, more appropriately in education, cost-effectiveness)? On the evidence available in October 2026, the answers are uneven. There is rigorous experimental evidence that some AI uses save teachers time on specific tasks without measurable loss of quality. There is experimental evidence that some AI designs improve short-term learning and that others harm it. There is very little published evidence that AI has produced realised cash savings in education budgets. An institution therefore cannot infer value from adoption, usage, or satisfaction; it has to establish it, use by use, against a named comparison.

Scope and terms. Return on investment (ROI) expresses monetised benefits as a ratio of costs. Cost-effectiveness analysis instead reports the cost of achieving a unit of a non-monetary outcome — for example, the cost per additional student reaching a mastery threshold — and is usually more appropriate in education, where learning cannot be converted into money without strong and contestable assumptions. The studies discussed below come from England, the United States, and Turkey; they concern specific tools, subjects, and age groups, and their results should not be assumed to transfer to other settings. This answer builds on two existing questions: Q089, which sets out how learning effects should be measured, and Q090, which sets the evidence standards to demand from vendors. Its distinctive task is to connect those measurements to money.

Financial savings: the outcome most often claimed and least often shown. A financial saving exists only when a budget line falls: fewer purchased hours, a cancelled contract, a post not refilled, lower overtime. Time saved is not money saved unless it is converted into one of these, and in education it rarely is, because freed teacher time is usually reabsorbed into other work. This research cycle found no published, independently evaluated study showing realised cash savings in a school or university budget attributable to AI. Claims of savings in vendor and commentary sources are typically projections — hours saved multiplied by a salary rate — rather than observed reductions in spending. Such projections can be useful for planning, but they describe potential capacity, not money the institution no longer spends. Where AI is used to justify an actual reduction in staffing or services, that is a workforce decision with educational consequences, examined in Q269.

Productivity gains: the strongest current evidence. The most rigorous evidence concerns teacher time. In the Education Endowment Foundation's randomised trial with 259 secondary science teachers in 68 English schools, teachers using ChatGPT alongside a guide spent 56.2 minutes a week on lesson and resource preparation, against 81.5 minutes in the comparison group — a saving of 25.3 minutes, or 31 per cent [SRC-1650]. An expert panel found that the quality of the resources did not appear to be affected, and the share of teachers who felt they spent too much time on planning fell from 49 to 26 per cent during the trial [SRC-1650]. The EEF's own summary and press coverage reported the result as cutting planning time by over 30 per cent [SRC-1176] [SRC-1680]. This is strong evidence of a specific productivity gain on a specific task, with a guide, in one subject and phase. It is not evidence of reduced total working hours, and it does not by itself say what teachers did with the time.

Survey evidence suggests larger gains among regular users. In Gallup's research with the Walton Family Foundation, teachers who used AI at least weekly — about three in ten — estimated saving an average of 5.9 hours a week, which the authors describe as equivalent to six weeks over a school year [SRC-1286] [SRC-1679]. These are self-reported estimates from adopters, who are likely to be those who benefit most, and they are not comparable in strength to the trial. The gap between 25 measured minutes on one task and nearly six self-reported hours across all tasks is itself informative: it marks the distance between what has been demonstrated and what is believed.

Educational value: benefit, harm, and design. Learning effects depend heavily on design. In a field experiment with nearly a thousand students in a Turkish high school, students given an unrestricted GPT-4 interface performed better on practice problems, but when access was removed they performed worse than students who never had it — about 17 per cent worse on the subsequent exam — while a version with teacher-designed safeguards largely avoided that harm [SRC-737]. An AI investment that raises practice scores while lowering independent performance would appear valuable on usage dashboards and harmful on the outcome that matters.

The most encouraging evidence for cost-effectiveness concerns AI that supports human tutors rather than replacing them. Tutor CoPilot, a system that offers live guidance to tutors during sessions, was evaluated in a randomised trial with tutors serving students in underserved communities in the United States. Students whose tutors had access were 4 percentage points more likely to master topics, and students of lower-rated tutors gained 9 percentage points, at an estimated cost of about $20 per tutor per year [SRC-1655] [SRC-1681] [SRC-1682]. Because the cost is small relative to the cost of tutoring itself, the implied cost per additional student reaching mastery is low. This is the clearest example in the current literature of an AI use whose educational value can be related to its cost. It remains a single trial in one subject and one programme model, and the original paper was released as a preprint before later publication [SRC-1655], which means its findings should be replicated before being generalised.

From effect to cost-effectiveness: a worked illustration. The arithmetic that connects an effect to a cost is simple; the inputs are what is hard. Suppose a tool costs a fixed sum per licence, and a trial shows that it raises the share of students reaching mastery by a certain number of percentage points. The cost per additional student reaching mastery is the total cost divided by the number of additional students who reach it. Two adjustments change that figure substantially in practice. The first is uptake: if only some licensed users use the tool, the cost per user served rises accordingly, and an effect measured among active users cannot be assumed across all licence holders. The second is full cost: if staff time for training, support, and supervision is excluded, the calculation flatters the tool. A cost-effectiveness figure that uses licence price and trial effects alone is a lower bound on cost and an upper bound on value. The same arithmetic should be applied to the alternatives — additional human tutoring hours, for example — so that comparisons are like for like.

Time horizons. Value also depends on when it is measured. Teacher time savings appear within weeks; learning effects may take a term or a year to show; effects on progression, attainment, or employment take years. Costs follow the opposite pattern: implementation costs arrive first, recurring costs continue, and exit costs arrive last. An evaluation that measures early benefits against early costs, or early costs against benefits projected far into the future, will misjudge value. A credible assessment specifies the horizon in advance and reports interim results as interim.

Labour-market evidence and the limits of extrapolation. Investments are sometimes justified by broader economic returns — that AI makes workers, including educators, more productive in ways that eventually show up in pay or output. The most rigorous large-scale evidence available, linking surveys to Danish administrative records across occupations exposed to AI chatbots, found precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours, ruling out effects larger than 2 per cent two years after ChatGPT's launch, while documenting the reorganisation of tasks [SRC-1683]. That study is a working paper and concerns the wider workforce, but it cautions against assuming that task-level time savings translate quickly into measurable economic gains.

What the demand side shows. Survey evidence also shows what teachers believe AI is worth. The Gallup–Walton research found that 60 per cent of US teachers used an AI tool for their work in the 2024–25 school year, while 40 per cent did not [SRC-1286] [SRC-1679]. Adoption at that level indicates perceived value among many users, but perceived value is not a substitute for measured value, and non-use by a large minority means that institution-wide licences buy access for many people who do not use it — a point that directly affects cost per active user (Q266).

What evidence would establish value. Drawing these strands together, an institution can establish whether a particular AI investment is worthwhile by meeting six conditions. It should state in advance which of the four outcomes — saving, productivity, educational value, or a combination — it expects, and how each will be measured. It should measure against a comparison group or a credible baseline, not against expectations. It should measure independent performance as well as assisted performance, so that harms of the kind found in Turkey are detected. It should record full costs, including staff time and exit (Q266), so that cost-effectiveness can be calculated. It should distinguish freed time from saved money, and record what freed time was used for. And it should prefer independent evaluation, peer-reviewed where possible, over vendor-reported results (Q090). Where those conditions are met, AI investments can be compared with other uses of the same money (Q267). Where they are not, claims of value — financial or educational — remain assertions.

Q269 How does AI adoption affect the size, workload, and compensation of the teaching workforce?

6 citations · 6 sources · 6 cross-references

Claim 1 emerging

AI is changing what some education staff spend their time on, but on the evidence available in October 2026 it has not been shown to reduce the number of people schools and universities employ, nor to change pay in any systematic way. The strongest evidence shows bounded, task-specific time savings — most clearly in lesson planning — alongside new work created by AI itself: checking outputs, adapting guidance, investigating suspected misuse, and learning tools that change frequently. The main financial risk to the workforce is therefore not that AI has already replaced staff, but that budget pressure may lead institutions to treat projected time savings as a justification for cutting or not filling posts before the evidence supports it.

Scope and terms. "Education workforce" here includes teachers, teaching assistants, support and administrative staff in schools, and academic and professional staff in universities, including contingent and adjunct faculty. Workforce size means numbers of posts and hours; roles means the tasks those posts contain; workload means time and intensity; pay means salaries and paid hours, including paid time for training. Evidence comes mainly from England, the United States, and Denmark. The costs of AI to institutions are examined in Q266, the financing choices in Q267, and the evidence of value in Q268.

What the evidence shows about time. The most rigorous education-specific evidence comes from the Education Endowment Foundation's trial in English secondary schools, which found that science teachers using ChatGPT with a guide reduced weekly lesson-planning time by about 31 per cent without an apparent loss of quality in the materials produced [SRC-1176]. In absolute terms the saving was about 25 minutes a week, on one task, for teachers who received guidance. Survey evidence from the United States points to larger self-reported savings among frequent users: in Gallup's research with the Walton Family Foundation, teachers who used AI at least weekly estimated saving about 5.9 hours a week, which the authors express as six weeks over a school year [SRC-1286] [SRC-1679]. The two figures measure different things — one a measured effect on a defined task, the other self-reported estimates by adopters across all tasks — and should not be combined. Together they support the conclusion that AI can relieve some workload for some staff; they do not show that the relief is large, universal, or durable.

What the evidence shows about jobs and pay. No study found in this research cycle shows that AI has reduced staffing levels in schools or universities. The broadest evidence on AI and employment comes from outside education. A large Danish study linking surveys to administrative records, covering occupations exposed to AI chatbots, found precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours two years after ChatGPT's launch, ruling out effects larger than 2 per cent, while documenting that employers reorganised tasks and that new work emerged around AI oversight and integration [SRC-1683]. That study is a working paper and covers the wider workforce, but its pattern — tasks change, totals do not yet — is consistent with the education evidence. It cautions against both the claim that AI is already displacing staff and the claim that it is already raising their pay.

New work created by AI. Time savings on one task are partly offset by new tasks elsewhere. Teachers and lecturers must verify AI-generated materials for accuracy; revise assessments that AI can complete; investigate suspected misuse; explain policies to students and parents; and learn tools that change with each model update. The Turkish high-school experiment showing that unrestricted AI access can lower students' independent performance [SRC-737] implies a further task: designing and supervising AI use so that learning is protected. This work is real, it is often unpaid in the sense that it is absorbed into existing hours, and it rarely appears in estimates of time saved. A net assessment of workload therefore needs time logs that record new tasks as well as faster old ones.

Teachers' perceptions and the role of institutional support. Survey evidence shows a divided workforce. The Gallup–Walton research found that about six in ten US teachers used AI tools in the 2024–25 school year, about three in ten weekly, and four in ten not at all [SRC-1286] [SRC-1679]. Walton reported that only 19 per cent of teachers said their school had an AI policy, and that teachers in schools with a policy reported larger time savings than those in schools without one [SRC-1679]. That finding is correlational, but it suggests that whatever relief AI offers depends partly on institutional investment in guidance and training — which costs money and staff time. Where training is expected but not paid, the cost of acquiring the "AI dividend" falls on staff themselves.

What happens to freed time. Where AI does save time, the financial and educational consequences depend on what happens next, and that is a management choice rather than a property of the technology. Freed time can be returned to staff as reduced workload, which may support wellbeing and retention; redirected to other teaching tasks, such as feedback, intervention, or planning for students with additional needs; or converted into reduced staffing, larger classes, or additional duties. Each choice has a different cost and a different effect. The EEF trial measured a reduction in planning time [SRC-1176], but it did not determine how schools would use the time, and the survey evidence is silent on the question. Institutions that do not decide explicitly will find the time reabsorbed by existing demands, which may be reasonable but should be recognised.

Budget pressure and the risk of premature substitution. The risk most relevant to finance is that projected savings become a reason for reductions. Institutional AI spending competes with staffing in the same budgets. During the renewal of the California State University system's OpenAI agreement, EdSource reported faculty opposition, with critics arguing that the money should be weighed against jobs on campuses facing budget pressure [SRC-1676]. That dispute did not concern AI replacing staff directly; it concerned AI spending competing with staff spending. Both dynamics can operate at once. An institution that buys AI tools and then, under financial pressure, leaves posts unfilled on the assumption that the tools will absorb the work is making a staffing decision on the basis of projections, not evidence. The measured savings so far — minutes per week on specific tasks — do not support that assumption.

Equity within the workforce. The effects of AI are unlikely to be evenly distributed. Adoption and support vary between schools; the book's analysis of teacher workload (Q212) reports lower AI adoption in rural than in suburban schools, and differences by school poverty level remain an indicator to monitor rather than an established finding. Within higher education, contingent and adjunct staff, who are often paid per course and have little control over workload, may be the first to be asked to use AI for marking or feedback without additional pay or training, and the least able to refuse. Teaching assistants and administrative staff, whose tasks are often more routine, may face greater pressure on their roles than classroom teachers. No study found in this research cycle measures these effects directly.

Schools and universities compared. The workforce consequences differ by sector. In schools, staffing is closely tied to pupil numbers, class-size norms, and statutory duties, which limits how far AI can alter headcount in the short term; the more immediate questions concern workload, training, and the roles of teaching assistants and administrative staff. In universities, staffing is more flexible and a larger share of teaching is done by contingent staff, so changes in how marking, feedback, and tutorial support are organised can translate more directly into changes in hours and contracts. Large institutional licences, such as CSU's systemwide agreement, also place AI spending in direct competition with academic staffing in public debate [SRC-1676]. In both sectors, the absence of published data on AI and staffing means that claims in either direction should be treated with caution.

Pay, training, and collective agreements. If AI changes what staff do, it raises questions that are normally settled through employment terms: whether time spent learning AI tools is paid; whether freed time is returned to staff, redirected to other teaching tasks, or used to justify larger classes; whether AI may be used to mark or monitor staff work; and how staff are consulted before AI is introduced into their roles. These are matters for policy and, where they exist, for collective agreements. The evidence summarised here does not settle them, but it does indicate that the benefits of AI to staff depend on how such questions are answered.

What evidence would change this assessment. Three kinds of evidence would materially change these conclusions. The first is education-specific data on staffing: posts cut, merged, or left unfilled with AI cited as a reason, collected systematically rather than anecdotally. The second is measured workload evidence across a full range of tasks, including marking and administration, that records new AI-related work alongside time saved. The third is longer-run labour-market evidence; the Danish findings cover the first two years after ChatGPT's launch [SRC-1683], and later effects may differ. Until such evidence exists, the cautious reading set out here should be preferred to confident claims in either direction.

Practical implications. Institutions should record and publish how AI is affecting staff time, using measured time logs rather than projections; pay for the training they expect; decide explicitly, and with staff, how freed time is to be used; monitor whether AI is being used to shift work onto contingent or support staff, or onto the schools least able to absorb it; ensure that any role change is planned, consulted on, and evaluated rather than assumed, and that new tasks created by AI are counted alongside those it shortens; and treat AI-related changes to roles, staffing, and pay as workforce decisions subject to the same consultation and scrutiny as any other. The evidence supports AI as a source of bounded, task-specific relief. It does not yet support treating it as a basis for staffing reductions.

Q270 What costs do parents and households bear as AI enters education, and how do those costs differ between families?

12 citations · 12 sources · 5 cross-references

Claim 1 emerging

As AI enters education, households pay in five ways: subscriptions to general-purpose or education-specific AI tools; the devices and internet connections those tools require; paid services that build AI into tutoring, test preparation, or homework help; parents' time, spent supervising, learning, and talking with children about the tools; and the non-monetary price of "free" services, paid in data and attention. The evidence on how large these costs are is thin. This research cycle found no nationally representative study of what households actually spend on AI for education, in the United States or elsewhere. What the evidence does show is narrower but important. List prices for consumer AI tools are low compared with human tutoring but not trivial for low-income families. Free tiers are widely used and are not equivalent to paid ones. In the United States, federal help with home internet costs ended in 2024. And survey data indicate that teenagers in lower-income households are more likely than those in higher-income households to do most of their schoolwork with chatbots' help. The central financial question for families is therefore not only "what does a subscription cost?" but "what does the school provide, what does it expect, and what happens to children whose families cannot pay?"

Scope and terms. This answer concerns parents and guardians of school-age children, mainly in kindergarten to grade 12 in the United States, where the most detailed recent survey data are available, with comparisons to other countries where evidence allows. Costs borne directly by students who pay for their own education, chiefly in higher education, are examined in Q271. Three categories are distinguished throughout: tools a school requires; tools it presents as optional; and tools that are formally optional but expected in practice, because assignments, grading norms, or peer use assume them. The last category is the one where costs move to households most quietly. Prices quoted below are list prices at the time of writing; consumer AI pricing changes frequently (Q266).

What families can buy, and at what price. Consumer AI tools are priced as monthly subscriptions with a free entry tier. In January 2026, OpenAI listed three paid consumer tiers in the United States: ChatGPT Go at $8 a month, Plus at $20, and Pro at $200, with Go priced locally in some other markets [SRC-1684]. OpenAI's pricing page states that paid plans are priced per user per month [SRC-1685], so a household with several children using separate accounts multiplies the cost. Education-specific products are often cheaper. Khan Academy's Khanmigo tutor costs $4 a month or $44 a year for learners and families; a family plan lets parents add children from a parent account and see their interactions, and teachers' tools are free [SRC-1686]. At these list prices, a year of ChatGPT Go costs $96, a year of Plus $240, and a year of Khanmigo $44–48 before tax. Families who combine a general assistant with a specialised tutor, or pay for several children, face higher totals. These sums are small compared with private human tutoring (Q129), but they recur, and for a household on a tight budget a recurring charge competes directly with food, transport, and rent.

"Free" is not the same as costless. Most families who use AI do not pay for it in money. Free tiers carry three kinds of cost. The first is quality: free versions typically run less capable models with tighter limits (Q187). The second is attention and data. When OpenAI launched Go worldwide, it announced plans to test advertising in the free tier and in Go in the United States, while keeping Plus, Pro, Business, and Enterprise ad-free [SRC-1684]. Under that model, the families who pay least are the ones whose children see advertising. The third is supervision. Paid education products sometimes include parental features — Khanmigo's family plan offers views of children's interaction history and moderation alerts [SRC-1686] — while general-purpose free tools are designed for adults and leave supervision entirely to the household. A family relying on free tools may pay in exposure and parental vigilance what another pays in cash.

Who uses AI for schoolwork, and how heavily. The best recent US evidence comes from Pew Research Center's surveys of teenagers aged 13 to 17 and their parents. In autumn 2024, 26 per cent of teens said they had used ChatGPT for schoolwork, double the 13 per cent of 2023. That measure did not differ by household income, but awareness did: 84 per cent of teens in households earning $75,000 or more had heard of ChatGPT, against 67 per cent in households earning under $30,000 [SRC-1466]. By autumn 2025, 54 per cent of teens said they had used chatbots for schoolwork help, and one in ten said they did all or most of their schoolwork with chatbots' help [SRC-1431]. Heavy reliance was uneven. One in five teens in households earning under $30,000 did all or most of their schoolwork with chatbots, as did a similar share in households earning $30,000–$75,000, compared with 7 per cent in higher-earning households [SRC-1687].

These figures need careful reading. They are self-reported, they do not say whether tools were free or paid, and they do not establish why lower-income teens rely more heavily on chatbots. One plausible explanation, consistent with Q192, is that chatbots substitute for help that better-off families get from parents with time and relevant knowledge, or from paid tutors; these data do not test it. The financial implication is nevertheless clear. If lower-income students lean most on AI, the quality, safety, and cost of the free tools available to them matter more to them than to anyone else. Heavier reliance is not automatically a benefit. In a randomised trial in a Turkish high school, students given unrestricted GPT-4 access did better on practice problems but worse on a later exam taken without AI [SRC-737]. A household that cannot pay for a tool designed to protect learning, and whose child uses a general-purpose tool heavily instead, may bear an educational cost that never appears as a price.

Parents' time and knowledge. Parents' time may be one of the largest household costs. Pew found that 51 per cent of parents said their teen used chatbots, while 64 per cent of teens reported doing so; about three in ten parents were unsure. Just over half of parents said they ever talked with their teen about chatbots. Parents earning $75,000 or more were more likely than those earning under $30,000 to have done so: 56 per cent against 43 per cent [SRC-1688]. Supervising AI use, checking outputs, learning the tools, and discussing acceptable use all take time that is unevenly available. Parents working irregular hours or several jobs, those with limited English or formal education, and single parents have less of it. Oversight features help only parents with the time and confidence to use them. Schools that assume parents will "monitor AI use at home" are assigning unpaid work that falls hardest on families least able to absorb it.

Devices and connectivity. AI tools presuppose a capable device and reliable internet, and in the United States public support for the second has shrunk. The federal Affordable Connectivity Program gave eligible households up to $30 a month off internet service (up to $75 on qualifying Tribal lands) and a one-time discount of up to $100 on a computer or tablet. The Federal Communications Commission reports that more than 20 million households enrolled, and that the monthly discount ended on 1 June 2024 because Congress provided no further funding [SRC-1689]. The programme was not designed for AI, but its end returned connectivity costs to the households least able to bear them, just as AI tools began to assume constant access. Schools issuing devices to every student offset part of this; families sharing one phone or depending on mobile data are disadvantaged when AI-supported homework assumes a laptop and broadband (Q190, Q193).

Required, optional, and expected in practice. In many US states, public schools face legal limits on charging families for required educational activities, so required AI tools are generally school-provided. The financial risk lies in the third category. Three things can each turn an optional purchase into an expected one without any formal decision: a teacher recommending a paid tool "for extra practice"; homework that is easier with a paid assistant; or grading shaped by the polish of AI-assisted work. This research cycle found no systematic data on how often schools recommend paid AI products to families. That is itself a gap, and districts could close it by recording such recommendations. Where schools provide no AI at all, AI does not leave students' lives — Pew's figures show widespread use regardless — but the decision, the cost, and the supervision move to each household.

Paid AI-enhanced services. Tutoring firms, test-preparation companies, and homework platforms increasingly incorporate AI. Families may pay for services whose added value from AI is asserted rather than demonstrated (Q090). AI can lower providers' costs: the Tutor CoPilot study estimated its AI guidance for human tutors cost about $20 per tutor per year to run [SRC-1655]. Whether such savings reach families as lower prices depends on competition, not technology. Parents choosing between a human tutor, an AI-enhanced service, and a low-cost AI tutor face the comparison institutions face in Q268: total cost against independent evidence of effect.

How costs differ between families. The same price lands differently on different households:

  • Number of children. Families with several children pay more under per-user pricing, though some products, like Khanmigo's family plan, are built around a parent account with linked children [SRC-1686].
  • Disability. Families of disabled children may gain from text-to-speech, summarisation, or translation that previously required paid assistive products, but only if tools meet accessibility standards. Otherwise they pay twice.
  • Language. Families speaking a language other than the school's may depend on AI translation and its reliability risks.
  • Country. Prices set in dollars weigh far more heavily where incomes are lower. OpenAI describes Go as a low-cost plan first introduced in India in August 2025 and later extended to 170 more countries, with localised pricing in some markets [SRC-1684]. Localised pricing narrows but does not remove the affordability gap between countries, and in many low- and middle-income settings the binding constraint remains the device and the connection.

What schools and public authorities can do. Schools can provide the tools they expect students to use, through district licences that typically cost less per student than consumer subscriptions; Khan Academy, for instance, sells district access to Khanmigo separately from its family plan [SRC-1686]. In the United States, federal guidance states that formula and discretionary grant funds may be used for AI-based instructional materials and tutoring when programme requirements are met [SRC-1673]. That makes it a lawful use of existing grants but adds no new money (Q267). Schools can also:

  • design homework that does not depend on paid tools (poverty-aware design, Q192);
  • state clearly which tools are required, which are optional, and which free alternatives are adequate;
  • lend devices and hotspots;
  • avoid grading that rewards access to premium tools.

None of this is free, but each step moves cost from the households least able to pay to institutions that can spread it.

Practical implications. Five criteria follow:

  1. Any AI tool needed for required work should be provided free.
  2. Recommendations of paid tools to families should be recorded and reviewed.
  3. Homework and assessment should not disadvantage students using only free tools.
  4. Parents should receive plain guidance on free options and realistic supervision, not an unfunded duty to monitor.
  5. Household costs — money, time, devices, connectivity — belong in every institutional analysis of AI. Costs that leave the school budget are not eliminated; they are transferred, and they fall hardest on families with the least.

Related questions: Q129, Q187, Q192, Q193, Q271

  • Indicators to monitor: consumer AI prices and free-tier limits; share of districts providing student AI tools; records of schools recommending paid AI products; Pew income gaps in teen chatbot reliance; home broadband affordability; the source behind Q192's 73% figure ([SRC-1243]).

Q271 What costs do students bear as AI enters education, and how might those costs affect affordability, debt, and participation?

11 citations · 11 sources · 4 cross-references

Claim 1 emerging

Students who pay for their own education meet the costs of AI in four ways: direct charges, whether personal subscriptions or fees folded into tuition and technology charges; the devices and connectivity that AI tools assume; the time needed to learn tools, verify their outputs, and adapt to changing assessment rules; and the quality and fairness costs that arise when an institution expects AI use but does not provide it. On the evidence available in October 2026, three conclusions are reasonably secure. First, use is close to universal among undergraduates in at least one major system, while institutional provision lags well behind: in the United Kingdom, 95 per cent of full-time undergraduates report using AI, but only 38 per cent say their institution provides AI tools. Second, institutional purchasing can deliver access at a small fraction of consumer list prices, as the California State University (CSU) system shows. Third, there is no evidence yet that AI costs have measurably changed student borrowing, and no evidence that paying for premium AI raises graduates' earnings. The central affordability question is therefore less about the size of any single subscription than about whether tools that are required, or expected in practice, are supplied by institutions or left to students to buy.

Scope and terms. This answer concerns students who bear education costs directly, chiefly undergraduates and postgraduates in higher education, with most evidence drawn from the United States and the United Kingdom. Costs that fall on families of school-age children are examined in Q270; the institutional side of the same transactions is examined in Q266 and Q267. As in Q270, three categories matter: tools a course requires; tools presented as optional; and tools that are optional on paper but expected in practice, because assignments, marking standards, or peer behaviour assume them. Prices are list prices at the time of writing and change often (Q266). Figures on student AI use are self-reported and come from surveys whose sampling and wording differ, so they should be compared with care.

What a degree's worth of AI costs at list price. Consumer AI is sold by monthly subscription per user [SRC-1685]. At OpenAI's January 2026 US prices — $8 a month for ChatGPT Go, $20 for Plus, and $200 for Pro [SRC-1684] — a year costs $96, $240, or $2,400, and a four-year degree costs $384, $960, or $9,600, before tax and assuming prices do not change. Students who combine a general assistant with a writing tool, a coding assistant, or a specialised study product pay more. Promotional offers aimed at students may lower these costs for a time; this edition did not verify any current offer, and temporary discounts should not be treated as a stable price. Set against tuition and living costs, the lower tiers are modest. They are not trivial for students already working long hours to stay enrolled, and they recur every month of every year. The highest tier is a different matter: it is priced for professional users, and a student who felt it necessary to compete would be paying a sum comparable to a significant share of other course costs.

Institutional provision: the largest lever on student cost. Whether students pay at all depends mainly on what their institution supplies. In HEPI's 2026 survey of 1,054 full-time UK undergraduates, conducted by Savanta in December 2025, 38 per cent said their institution provides AI tools [SRC-1145], up from 23 per cent in 2025 and 9 per cent in 2024; about half of students think institutions should provide them [SRC-1690]. ChatGPT was the tool most often named as institutionally provided, followed by Microsoft Copilot, and some respondents specified the premium version of ChatGPT [SRC-1690]. HEPI recommends that institutions ensure that AI tools necessary or advantageous for course content or assessment are accessible to all students [SRC-1145]. The survey was sponsored by Kortext, a company that sells AI-enabled study products; that interest does not invalidate the findings, which come from a weighted sample with a margin of error of about 3 per cent, but readers should know it.

The CSU case shows the scale of the price difference that institutional purchasing can achieve. CSU states that its renewed agreement with OpenAI costs $1.60 per user, against a next-closest bid of $8.00 per user per month, and that coverage expands from 500,000 to 675,000 users, including graduates for a year after graduation; CSU's own analysis described the alternative as provision "supported by campus resources or a user fee" [SRC-1675]. The renewal was reported at $13 million a year for three years [SRC-1677]. On those figures, the per-user cost is roughly one-fifth of the $8 consumer price of ChatGPT Go and less than a tenth of the $20 Plus price, although the precise unit of the $1.60 figure is inferred from published totals rather than disclosed as a contract term (Q267). The saving to students is real; the cost has not disappeared. It has moved to a public budget, where faculty opponents argued during the renewal that the money should protect jobs on campuses facing austerity [SRC-1676]. A universal licence also pays for students who never use the tool, and the graduate allowance ends after a year, at which point former students face consumer prices or lose access. Institutional provision is therefore a transfer of cost and a choice of dependence, not a free good.

Fees as a hidden channel. Institutions that do not buy systemwide licences may still recover AI costs from students, through technology fees, programme fees, or course charges. Spreading costs this way protects students who could not afford a subscription, but it also charges students who do not use the tools and can make the price of AI invisible at the point of enrolment. This research cycle found no systematic data on how often institutions have introduced or raised fees specifically to fund AI. Q267's maintenance note already lists the introduction of AI user fees as an indicator to monitor; a fee that appears without a published rationale should be treated as a transfer of institutional cost to students and examined accordingly.

Unequal use and unequal readiness. Use is high but not uniform. HEPI found that students from households where the chief income earner works in a managerial or professional role were more likely than students from households where the earner works in a manual role or is unemployed to use some AI tools: 59 per cent against 51 per cent for generating text, 20 against 13 per cent for writing code, and 19 against 13 per cent for data analysis [SRC-1145]. HEPI notes that the driver is unclear, given that a free version of ChatGPT exists, and that cost is not always the underlying barrier [SRC-1145]. That caution matters: the gap may reflect familiarity, subject mix, confidence, or time as much as price, and the survey cannot separate them. A further finding points to readiness rather than money. HEPI reports that a third of students arrive in higher education without prior experience of using AI, and warns that without structured support they risk being disadvantaged [SRC-1145]. US data on teenagers suggest that students also arrive with very different habits: lower-income teens are more likely than higher-income teens to say they do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbots' help [SRC-1687]. Learning to use AI well, and unlearning dependence, is a cost paid in time, and it falls most heavily on students with the least of it.

Student aid and the cost of attendance in the United States. In the US, federal need-based aid is calculated against a student's cost of attendance (COA), which caps the total aid a student may receive under several federal programmes and enters the Pell Grant calculation. The Federal Student Aid Handbook states that only costs listed in section 472 of the Higher Education Act may be included. One listed category covers books, course materials, supplies, and equipment "required of all students in the same program," including a reasonable allowance for renting or buying a personal computer; another covers disability-related expenses, including equipment and services not provided by other agencies [SRC-1691]. This edition found no federal guidance that addresses AI subscriptions specifically. A reasonable reading is that an AI tool required of all students in a programme could fall within the course-materials category, and that a disability-related assistive AI tool could fall within the disability allowance, while optional or merely expected tools probably fall outside the COA. That reading is an interpretation, not settled guidance, and institutions should confirm it with their financial aid office or the Department of Education before relying on it. Including a cost in the COA also does not mean it is paid for: it raises the ceiling on aid, which for many students means more eligibility for loans rather than more grant money.

Debt. No study was found that links AI costs to student borrowing in any country. Compared with tuition and housing, lower-tier subscriptions are small, so any effect on total debt is likely to be marginal for most students; that is an inference from relative magnitudes, not a measured result. The risk is concentrated at the margins: students who finance everyday costs on credit, students close to aid limits, and students for whom a recurring charge competes with food or transport. Where a required tool is excluded from the COA, or where a programme quietly expects premium tools, students at those margins bear the cost without any corresponding aid.

Returns: what students are told AI will pay back. Purchases are often justified by future earnings: students are told that AI skills will be essential at work. HEPI found that 68 per cent of UK undergraduates believe AI skills are essential to thrive in today's world, while fewer than half (48 per cent) feel their teaching staff are helping them develop those skills [SRC-1145]. The labour-market evidence so far is more cautious than the expectation. The most rigorous large-scale study available, linking surveys to Danish administrative records, found precise null effects of AI chatbots on earnings and recorded hours across exposed occupations, ruling out effects larger than 2 per cent two years after ChatGPT's launch [SRC-1683]. That study covers workers rather than students, and later effects may differ. It does mean that no current evidence supports telling students that paying for premium AI is an investment with a measurable financial return.

Learning quality: paying more is not the same as learning more. The educational cost of AI does not track its price. In a field experiment in a Turkish high school, students given an unrestricted GPT-4 interface performed better on practice problems but worse on a later exam taken without AI, while a version with teacher-designed safeguards avoided the harm [SRC-737]. Although that study concerned secondary students, its lesson applies directly to higher education: a student who pays for the most capable general-purpose tool may learn less than one using a constrained tool designed for study. Institutions that provide tools have the opportunity to choose or configure them for learning; students left to buy their own usually choose on convenience and price.

Participation and assessment. AI also affects participation through assessment. HEPI reports that 65 per cent of UK undergraduates say assessment has changed significantly in response to AI, and that some students express anxiety about being falsely accused of misconduct [SRC-1145]. Each direction carries a cost. Where assessments assume AI use, students without capable tools or prior experience are disadvantaged. Where assessments ban AI but detection is unreliable, students bear the risk of investigations, delays, and appeals, a cost no survey has yet measured. HEPI's suggestion of combining AI-free assessments with assessments that expect AI use, together with practice before assessment, is one way to limit both risks [SRC-1145].

How costs differ between students. The same price falls differently on different students:

  • Part-time, adult, and community-college students often work and study at once, have the least slack in their budgets and time, and are under-represented in the surveys cited here.
  • Disabled students may gain from AI transcription, summarisation, or text-to-speech, and in the US may have assistive costs recognised in the COA disability allowance [SRC-1691], but only if tools are accessible.
  • International students and students in lower-income countries face prices set in dollars; OpenAI's localised Go pricing in some markets narrows but does not close the gap [SRC-1684].
  • Graduates lose institutional access when licences end, as in CSU's one-year graduate allowance [SRC-1675], at the point when their incomes are often lowest.

Alternatives, including not providing AI. Institutions have four broad options. They can provide nothing, in which case use continues — HEPI's 95 per cent figure shows that — but students self-supply unequally. They can buy institutional licences, which reduce per-student cost and allow tools to be configured for learning, at the price of budget commitment and vendor dependence. They can recover costs through fees, which spreads costs but hides them. Or they can design courses and assessments so that free or open tools are sufficient, which limits student cost but may limit what courses can teach. These options can be combined, and each should be compared on full cost, educational effect, and distribution (Q268).

Practical implications. Seven criteria follow:

  1. Any AI tool required for a course or its assessment should be provided at no additional charge, or listed openly as a course cost and reflected in the cost of attendance where the rules allow.
  2. Institutions should publish expected AI-related costs before enrolment, distinguishing required, optional, and expected tools.
  3. Assessments should be designed so that students using only free or institution-provided tools are not disadvantaged.
  4. Structured induction should be offered to students arriving without AI experience.
  5. Any fee that funds AI should be itemised and justified.
  6. Licences should include a plan for students' transition at graduation.
  7. Institutions should collect data on student AI spending, by income and background, so that affordability can be monitored rather than assumed.
